How product marketers use AI

Most PMMs are using AI at the chat level. Here is what it looks like when you run it through your actual workflows instead.

Will Mulholland · 17 August 2026 · 12 minute read

Key points

  • 80% of PMMs now use AI regularly, but most are at the chat level, not the workflow level (PMA 2026 State of Product Marketing).
  • The six core use cases: customer research, competitive intelligence, positioning and messaging, launch planning, sales enablement, and content production.
  • Context is the real differentiator. PMMs getting the most out of AI have loaded their tools with customer language and product knowledge, not just sent one-off prompts.
  • You do not need to code. The best first move is picking one task you hate and running it through a context-loaded project.
  • The confidentiality concern is real and solvable. Team plans, the API path, and local models each give you a clean answer depending on sensitivity.
  • Positioning, judgment, and final brand voice still sit with the product marketer. AI handles the first draft; you make it true.

The gap between dabbling and operating

If you search how product marketers use AI right now, you will find a lot of listicles telling you AI can help with "content creation, competitive research, and customer insights." That is accurate and also completely useless if you are trying to figure out where to actually start.

Here is the more honest version: most product marketers use AI the same way everyone else does. They open ChatGPT, type something in, get a response, then spend twenty minutes editing it into something acceptable. That is dabbling. It saves a little time. It does not change anything fundamental about how the job works.

The PMMs getting real value from AI are doing something different. They have built up context over time. Their positioning history, their customer language, their ICP, their product differentiators, the top objections the sales team hears. All of it lives in a project that AI can work from. So when they run a competitive summary, a messaging draft, or a launch brief, the output is grounded in their specific situation, not generic advice.

According to the Product Marketing Alliance's 2026 State of Product Marketing Report, 80% of PMMs now use AI regularly, with adoption clustering in three areas: drafting copy, summarising research, and producing first-draft assets. That tells you where most people are. The question is what it takes to go further.

Why it matters now

The speed advantage is compounding. A competitive summary that used to take half a day now takes 20 minutes with a good setup. A messaging doc that sat in draft for two weeks because nobody had time to start it now has a solid first pass before lunch. That time comes back, and the PMMs who use it on strategic work, the customer calls, the cross-functional alignment, the positioning bets, are pulling ahead of the ones doing everything manually.

The bigger shift is structural. As AI handles more of the first-draft and research work, what remains distinctly valuable in a PMM role is judgment: what to say, to whom, and why it will land. The people who spend more time on that judgment, because AI absorbed the mechanical work, are going to be harder to replace than the ones still spending four hours building a battlecard from scratch.

Six ways product marketers use AI day to day

1. Customer and market research

Customer research has always been slow. You run interviews, transcribe them, read pages of notes, look for patterns, write a synthesis two weeks after anyone needed it. The actual insight is usually buried in the fourth paragraph of the seventh interview.

AI compresses the timeline without changing the quality bar. Paste a set of interview transcripts, G2 reviews, or support tickets into a context-loaded project, ask it to surface recurring pain points and the language customers use to describe them, and you have a usable starting point in minutes. Not a replacement for reading the source material yourself, but a strong first pass that tells you where to focus.

The more useful setup is a project in Claude where your existing customer research lives permanently. Every new interview you add gets interpreted through what you already know. Over time, the context compounds rather than restarting with every new prompt.

2. Competitive intelligence

Competitive research is the PMM task most people procrastinate on. Important, ongoing, and tedious. You have to check competitor sites, read release notes, monitor pricing changes, scan G2 reviews, and synthesise what it all means before the next sales meeting. Most PMMs skip the monitoring entirely between major reviews because it is too slow to be a regular habit.

AI handles the first layer well. It can summarise a competitor's homepage, extract positioning claims from a product page, and compare feature language across multiple sites in the time it used to take to read one. That changes the cadence from "quarterly check" to "15 minutes a week."

The better version is a project pre-loaded with your battlecard template, your positioning, and your product's actual differentiators. When you paste in a competitor update, the output is already framed against your context. Not generic analysis, but a draft answer to "how do we position against this?"

3. Positioning and messaging

This is where most people try AI first and most people are disappointed. They send a one-line prompt and expect a complete positioning statement back. The output is generic because the input was generic.

What works is building context before you ask the question. Your target customer, their pain points in their own words, what they believe about the problem before they find your product, what they have tried before, what success looks like for them. Once that context is in, the conversation shifts from "write me a positioning statement" to "here is my context, here is my current draft, here are the objections the sales team keeps hearing, help me tighten the differentiation."

That second version is useful. The first version produces a paragraph that could belong to any company in your category.

4. Launch planning

Product launches involve a predictable set of deliverables: a brief, a timeline, a messaging doc, internal comms, external content, sales enablement, and metrics to track. The structure is the same every time. The content changes.

AI is well suited to this because the pattern is reusable. Once you have a launch brief template and a set of prompts that work, you can produce a first-pass launch plan in under an hour. Not the finished version, but the skeleton that takes two hours off every subsequent conversation and gives cross-functional partners something to react to rather than a blank doc.

The less obvious use case is the post-mortem. After a launch, paste your original brief, the metrics you hit, and feedback from the sales team into a project with one question: what would we do differently? The synthesis from that conversation is often more honest than the post-mortem meeting.

5. Sales enablement

One-pagers, battlecards, objection-handling guides, discovery question banks. These are the deliverables PMMs are always behind on and that sit between "nice to have" and "blocking deals."

AI removes the blank page. Feed in your positioning, your product's key differentiators, the top objections the sales team hears, and a competitor profile. You get a first-draft battlecard in twenty minutes. The job is editing for truth rather than creating from scratch. That is a different kind of work, faster and less draining.

For teams with Gong or Chorus in the stack, the call intelligence data is the best raw material you have. These tools tag recurring objections, product confusion, and competitor mentions across hundreds of calls automatically. Pull that data into your AI project before writing any sales asset and the output reflects what customers actually say, not what you think they say.

6. Content production

The most common AI use case for PMMs is also the most visible: content. Blog posts, social updates, product update emails, case study drafts, newsletter issues.

The risk is not that AI makes you less creative. It is that it makes it too easy to produce generic output at scale. Your voice gets flattened. Every competitor's content starts to sound the same. This is already happening across every feed, and readers can tell.

The version that works is using AI for structure and first draft while you own the point of view. The specific claim that is only true for your product. The contrarian angle that only makes sense given what you know about your customers. The insight from actually talking to people. You write that bit. AI handles the rest.

The confidentiality problem and how to solve it

Most PMMs hit the same wall when they try to use AI for real work: the information they need to include is sensitive. Customer names, unannounced roadmap items, internal pricing, sales call recordings. The "just paste it into Claude" advice breaks down immediately when the context is anything you would not want on a company blog.

This is a real concern, not a reason to stop. Three paths through it:

For most internal context, team plans are the simplest answer. Anthropic's Claude Team plan and OpenAI's ChatGPT Team plan both have explicit commitments not to train models on your conversations. That covers positioning work, competitive research, internal briefs, and most day-to-day PMM tasks without additional setup.

For more sensitive work involving customer data or legal exposure, the API path gives you full control. You call the model directly and nothing goes into a shared pool. This requires some initial setup but is well-documented.

For anything involving PII or data that cannot move outside your infrastructure, a local model running on your machine (Ollama with Llama 3, for example) processes everything without data leaving your device. More friction, but complete control.

Most PMMs can handle 90% of their use cases with the first option. Start there.

Common mistakes

Sending prompts without context is the most common one. AI outputs are only as useful as the inputs. A one-line prompt gets a one-size-fits-all response. A prompt with your customer context, your positioning, and your specific question gets something you can build on.

Using AI to write things you have not thought through yet is the second. If you do not know what you are trying to say, AI will produce something that sounds right but probably is not. Clarity of thought still has to come from you first. AI expresses a clear idea clearly. It cannot generate clarity where there is none.

Treating the first output as the deliverable is the third. The first draft is a starting point. The useful work is in the iterations: pushing on the parts that feel generic, asking for a different angle, pressure-testing the logic against what you actually know about your customers.

Frequently asked questions

What AI tools do product marketers actually use?

Claude and ChatGPT are the two most common for writing and analysis. Gong and Chorus for call intelligence, which surfaces recurring objections and competitor mentions across sales calls. Granola for meeting notes. Perplexity for research. Most PMMs run four or five tools rather than one because no single platform covers positioning, research, decks, customer interviews, and content production well.

Which PMM task should I use AI for first?

Pick the one you are most behind on and most reluctant to start. For most people that is competitive research or the first draft of a messaging doc. Start there, spend an hour building the context once, and use it repeatedly. Every subsequent run is faster and more grounded than the first.

How do I stop AI output from sounding generic?

Put your voice in before you ask for the output. Real customer quotes in their own words. Your actual point of view on the market. The specific objections your sales team hears on calls. The claims that are true for your product and not every other product in the category. AI produces generic output when it has nothing specific to work from. Give it something specific.

Do I need to know how to code?

No. Everything in this guide runs in a standard Claude or ChatGPT interface. No terminal, no APIs, no code. The more advanced confidentiality options, like local models or API access, involve some setup, but there are step-by-step guides for both.

Will using AI make my positioning generic?

Only if your input is generic. Positioning work done with a well-loaded context project is grounded in your specific customer language, your actual differentiators, and the real market you are operating in. The positioning work is still yours. AI removes the blank page.

How long does it take to get value from this?

Setting up a basic project in Claude or ChatGPT with your core context loaded takes under two hours. Most PMMs see meaningful time savings in the first week. The compounding starts once the context project becomes your default starting point for any PMM task rather than something you return to occasionally.

Can I use AI for work involving customer data?

Yes, with the right account type. Claude and ChatGPT team plans do not train on your conversations. For more sensitive data, the API path or a local model are both viable. The confidentiality section above covers each option and when to use it.
